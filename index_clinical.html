Act 01 · Endoscopic viewport

MediMorph

A clinical digital twin of a 4D-printed, dual-stimuli shape-memory stent for the gastrointestinal tract, from the physiology it must survive to the computation that designs it for one patient.

Scroll to advance the endoscope
Physiological environment

A hostile, restless lumen

The implant sits in acid. Gastric pH swings between 1.5 and 3.5, then climbs to 6.5–7.5 past the pylorus, with bile salts and proteases on top. That gradient degrades coatings and polymers at different rates along a single device.

The wall never stops moving. Peristaltic waves apply cyclic multiaxial fatigue (radial compression, axial shear and torsion at once), several million cycles per year. Watch the telemetry: every wave is a load case.

Gen 11992

Bare Metal Stent

material
316L stainless steel · Co-Cr
architecture
slotted-tube diamond cells
failure mode
In-stent restenosis 20–30 %

Rigid metal cages the wall permanently. The tissue reads the cage as a chronic injury and neointimal hyperplasia grows through the cells. In the viewport the mucosa closes over the struts and the lumen narrows again.

Gen 22002

Drug-Eluting Stent

platform
metal + polymer carrier
payload
anti-proliferative (sirolimus-class)
failure mode
late stent thrombosis

A polymer coating elutes anti-proliferative drugs that suppress restenosis. The same drugs delay healing over the struts, so exposed metal and polymer remain thrombogenic for months or years. Watch for the clot forming on the struts.

Gen 32012

Bioresorbable Scaffold

material
PLLA · magnesium alloys
strut thickness
≈ 2× metallic
failure mode
early recoil · fracture

The scaffold is meant to dissolve once its job is done. Polymer and magnesium are weaker than steel, so the struts have to be thick, the lumen recoils early and the lattice can fracture under cyclic load before it has finished resorbing.

Diagnosis · structural stasis

Why static generations are no longer viable

All three generations are structurally static after deployment. They keep one geometry and one radial force from the day they are implanted, whether that is metal, drug-coated metal or a polymer that only weakens with time.

  • ✕  cannot follow peristaltic deformation → fatigue, migration
  • ✕  cannot respond to pH and remodeling → restenosis, recoil
  • ✕  cannot change shape to be removed → traumatic retrieval

The physiology is dynamic, so the implant has to be dynamic too.

Act 02 · Cutaway twin

The Stenting Trilemma

Every static stent is a frozen compromise between three coupled trade-offs, and improving one axis costs another. Drag the design point in the instrument panel: the twin re-solves wall contact, anchoring and curvature live.

Trade-off 01

Radial Force vs Tissue Trauma

The stent has to push outward hard enough to hold a stricture open against compression. Past a threshold, that contact pressure causes ischemia, ulceration and hyperplasia. The heat map on the wall shows the pressure field.

Trade-off 02

Anti-Migration vs Safe Retrieval

Flared ends, barbs and uncovered segments anchor the device against peristaltic drag. They also give tissue something to grow into, and the anchoring that prevents migration then makes extraction traumatic.

Trade-off 03

Deliverability vs Conformability

A low-profile, pushable system crosses tight, tortuous anatomy, but an axially stiff device straightens a curved lumen and loads its edges. A soft, conformable lattice follows the curve, but is harder to deliver and holds less force.

A static stent stays at one point of this triangle for its whole service life. A 4D stent can move through the triangle over time: press “Show 4D path”.

Act 03 · Fabrication

4D Printing

4D printing is 3D printing plus time. The lattice is printed layer by layer from a shape-memory polymer, and the material itself carries the instructions for how it should move after implantation.

Smart polymers

Netpoints and switching segments

A shape-memory polymer network has two parts. Netpoints (crosslinks or hard domains) remember the permanent shape. Switching segments freeze below the transition temperature Ttrans and become mobile above it. Heating above Ttrans drops the modulus by orders of magnitude and the material turns rubbery.

Programming · heat & crimp

Crimped above Ttrans

The printed stent is heated above its transition temperature and crimped onto the delivery catheter while rubbery. The switching chains stretch and align, storing entropy like a loaded spring. The 3D chart traces the cycle in stress–strain–temperature space.

Programming · fix & recover

Fixed in a temporary shape

Cooling under constraint vitrifies the switching segments. After the load is released, the crimped shape stays: that is shape fixity Rf. Reheating above Ttrans releases the stored entropy and the lattice returns to its printed form, which is shape recovery Rr.

Act 04 · Dual-stimuli actuation

Delivered cold, crimped, silent

The programmed scaffold travels through the endoscope below Ttrans, kept glassy by a chilled flush. Scroll to run the sequence, or take the console and drive the temperature yourself.

Stimulus 1 · thermal

37 °C: autonomous expansion

At body temperature the switching segments soften and the lattice recovers its programmed diameter on its own, with no balloon and no metal spring. The radial force is set by the polymer network, so wall contact stays in the atraumatic band.

The recovery is one-way: cooling afterwards does not re-crimp the stent. Try it on the console.

Stimulus 2 · near-infrared

808 nm: remote, local heat

NIR light at 808 nm sits in the tissue optical window. Photothermal fillers in the polymer turn it into heat inside the scaffold only, taking it past a second switching temperature T₂ ≈ 45 °C without heating the surrounding wall.

Atraumatic retrieval

Contraction away from the wall

The second programmed shape is smaller. The lattice contracts and pulls away from the mucosa, so contact pressure falls to zero and a clearance gap opens. The device can then be withdrawn without tearing embedded tissue.

Act 05 · Computational inverse design

Hyperelastic FEA

Above Ttrans the polymer behaves as a hyperelastic solid. Uniaxial and biaxial test data are fitted with Yeoh and Mooney–Rivlin strain-energy functions and fed into a finite-element model of the full lattice. The stent is colored by von Mises stress.

Physics-informed neural networks

Physics as a loss function

A PINN learns the displacement and stress fields while being penalized for violating equilibrium, boundary conditions and the constitutive law. It can then evaluate a candidate design in milliseconds instead of hours.

Patient-specific mapping

Designed backwards from the patient

The patient's lumen is segmented from CT slices into a point cloud. The network inverts the problem: it looks for the cell count, strut width and flare profile that put contact pressure in the safe band across that specific anatomy.
